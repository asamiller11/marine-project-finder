What it does

The tool reads public environmental assessment and permit notices for marine construction projects, such as ports, wharves, breakwaters, dredging and shoreline works. It flags projects that may need marine mitigation, for example avoidance and reduction measures, habitat enhancement, fish habitat offsetting, or nature-inclusive design. Each listing links to the official notice.

What the score and tiers mean

Each notice gets a score from 0 to 100 from simple, visible rules: the type of marine work, words about fish habitat, offsetting, eelgrass, shellfish or species at risk, and the size of the work. The score places each notice in a tier:

  • Strong fit: Score 60 or more. Several signals of marine mitigation, such as offsetting, new habitat or large in-water works.
  • Possible fit: Score 35 to 59. Some signals. Worth reviewing.
  • Weak fit: Score 20 to 34. Few signals. Often routine maintenance.

Notices scoring under 20, quarries and pits, and withdrawn projects are not listed at all. Only strong and possible fits are shown at first; weak fits can be switched on with the "Also show weak fits" box. A high score means the notice is worth a closer look. It does not mean that mitigation or compensation is required. Only the regulator decides that.

Where a source's terms allow it, each listing shows short quotes: complete sentences copied exactly from the notice, chosen because they describe the project and its habitat signals, so you can judge for yourself. Where they do not (at the moment, Scotland's marine licensing site), the listing shows short facts, the words that matched and a link to the official notice instead. US Federal Register notices also show no quotes: they are mostly about marine mammals and noise, so their cards show only what the notice says about the construction, and a link.

How winnable a project is

The score has two parts. The first (above) asks whether the notice describes the right kind of work. The second adds or takes away points for what decides whether a supplier can still get in: the stage (pre-application, open for comment and under review score higher than approved or under construction), whether habitat offsetting, compensation or a Fisheries Act authorization is mentioned, whether an engineering or environmental consultant is named, when construction is due to start, and the size of the work. This second part changes a project's tier but never adds a project to the list or takes one off it. Every project shows a "Best way in" line (who to approach, and when) and two or three plain reasons for its score.

Who is involved

Each project lists its owner or proponent, its engineering or environmental consultant, its contractor and the agency contact, but only when the official source names them, with a note of where the name came from. Nothing is guessed. A role the source does not name shows "Not named yet": contractors, for example, are usually named only when a project goes to bid. For agency contacts only the name, job title and office are shown; phone numbers and email addresses are on the official notice. "Download shown projects (CSV)" includes these names, for building a target list.

For Strong and Possible projects, names and facts are also taken from officially published web pages: the developer's project site, press releases, procurement notices and environmental reports. Each is linked to its source; facts found only through a web search, on sites that do not allow automated reading, say so. Procurement routes (supplier portals, tenders, meet-the-buyer events) appear first, under "Best way in". "Find contacts" opens a LinkedIn people search for the organisation (environmental manager, project manager, procurement); this site stores nothing from it. Projects marked "From the developer's website, not in a permit notice yet" come from a developer's own pages and are scored with the same rules.

Offshore wind counts under the hard-structure profile: turbine foundations need scour protection and cables need cable protection. Fixed-bottom foundations score higher than floating ones. Approved projects can be at most Possible, and projects under construction at most Weak.

Automatic listings and review

Every scored notice is published automatically. Under "Sources", each listing says either "Listed automatically; not yet checked by a person" or, once a person has checked it against the official notice, "Checked by a person". Listings found to be wrong or irrelevant on review are removed. Summaries are kept short and factual, and facts that a notice does not give are shown as "unknown".

When the same project appears in two registries, it is listed once, with a link to the other listing. Listings whose official page cannot be found are left out.

Notices in other languages

Quotes always stay in the notice's original language. There is no automatic translation. For notices not in English, the English summary is built only from the keywords and sizes the tool found in the text, and is labelled "built from keywords, not a translation". When too little was found, the listing says "Not translated" and shows the original-language quotes instead. The keyword rules for French, Danish, Spanish, Norwegian and Dutch have fewer tests than the English rules, so those listings are lower confidence and carry a "Less tested language" badge.

Public comments

Each project shows when its public comment period opens and closes and how many days are left, but only when the official registry publishes those dates. When an opening date is taken from the date of the notice that invited comments, it is labelled "date of the notice". Where a registry publishes the number of comments received, the number is shown with the date it was read and a link to the registry's own comments page. Comment text, the names of people who commented and other personal details are never copied to this site. Anything a source does not publish is shown as "Not published".

While a comment period is open, the project carries an "Open for public comment until" label with the closing date. The label disappears once that date has passed. "Public comment deadlines coming up" at the top of the project list, the "Only projects open for public comment" filter and the RSS feeds all use the same dates.

  • Canadian Impact Assessment Registry: The registry shows the number of comments received on some project pages (mostly larger assessments).
  • Newfoundland and Labrador environmental assessment: The registry publishes the comment deadline but not the number of comments received.
  • Høringsportalen, Denmark's national consultation portal (Atom feeds): The portal's feeds give the hearing deadline but not the number of responses.
  • marine.gov.scot marine licence documentation: Representations are published as PDF documents, not as a count.
  • Boletín Oficial del Estado (BOE) open data: BOE notices give the comment period in working days and publish no count.

Changes between updates and project pages

Every project has its own page with a stable address. At each update the site keeps a short snapshot (deadline, stage and tier) and marks projects that are new, whose comment deadline changed, or that are now decided. Each project page shows a timeline of those changes next to the official comment dates.

Buyer profiles

At the top of the project list you can choose your type of marine mitigation work ("I work on: ..."). The list then uses that profile's tiers and order, and each project says in plain words why it matches. "All marine mitigation work" is the general score described above and is the default.

  • Hard-structure eco-engineering: Seawalls, bulkheads, piers, wharves, breakwaters, armouring and riprap, where eco-engineered units could replace plain concrete or rock.
  • Living shoreline and shellfish: Oysters, mussels, salt marsh, shoreline stabilization and erosion control.
  • Seagrass and eelgrass: Eelgrass and seagrass beds: surveys, transplants, restoration and compensation.
  • Kelp and other seaweed: Kelp forests, rockweed and other seaweed, and the rocky reefs they grow on.
  • Coral: Warm- and cold-water coral, coralline habitat and sponge reefs. No current projects match. The current sources rarely describe this habitat.
  • Mitigation banking and compensation: Offsetting, compensation, credits, habitat banking and compensation plans.

Profiles are rule-based estimates. Each one re-weights the same visible rules (keywords, project types and sizes) toward one kind of work, using the same Strong, Possible and Weak cut-offs. A project is listed for a profile only if the notice mentions at least one of that profile's core subjects. A project that the general list leaves out appears under a profile only if it reaches Possible (35 or more) there. A flag under any profile means the notice may be relevant to that kind of work. It is not a statement that mitigation, compensation or any particular measure is required.

Profiles read notices a little more strictly than the general score: a keyword does not count when the same clause negates it or makes it conditional ("does not propose changes to the wharf", "if required"), sizes taken from sentences about past works or distances ("within 250 m of the site") are not counted, and for the hard-structure profile, small routine repairs (under 100 m long, 2,000 m² and 5,000 m³) stay Weak unless the notice also mentions marine mitigation. Your profile choice is stored only in your browser. You can share a profile with a link such as ?profile=seagrass.

Project stage and leads

For comment periods, a project whose comment period has closed or that has been decided is marked "Likely too late". For product makers (the hard-structure profile and its shortlist) that label is replaced by the project's stage: under assessment (comments open or closed) or approved. An approved project has passed environmental review; it is still shown as a lead because design and purchasing may still be ahead, but it scores lower than a project that is still under review (see How winnable a project is). The site marks a project as under construction only when the notice says so or its works window has started; it cannot tell whether a tender has been issued, so treat "Approved, may not be built yet" as a prompt to check.

Projects on lakes or rivers carry a "Freshwater" label. They are hidden from the hard-structure profile unless you tick "Include freshwater". Hydroelectric stations and dams, and maintenance dredging of an existing berth with no new structure, are not treated as marine habitat projects.

Terms used in notices

  • SCH (Small Craft Harbour): a fishing or recreational harbour run by Fisheries and Oceans Canada.
  • Intent to Make a Determination: the federal authority plans to decide whether the project is likely to cause significant adverse environmental effects. The public can comment before it decides.

Your watch list

"Add to watch list" saves a project in your own browser only. There are no accounts, and nothing about your watch list is sent anywhere. Clearing your browser data clears the list.

Project pages we follow

Some sources list projects rather than notices. When such a source is used, its items appear in a separate "Project page we follow (not a notice)" section, with no comment deadline, no countdown, and never in the public comment deadline list. No project watch source is in use at the moment.

How often it updates

The list is refreshed every week, and new notices appear on their own. Comment countdowns are calculated in your browser, so they stay current between updates.

RSS feeds

Each feed lists the public comment deadlines in the next 14 days, then the projects that are new since the last update, with a link to the official notice and the source. Add a feed address to any feed reader. There is no sign-up and no email: this site stores nothing about who reads the feeds.

  • Public comment deadlines in the next 14 days (every profile)
  • All projects on the general list
  • Hard-structure eco-engineering
  • Living shoreline and shellfish
  • Seagrass and eelgrass
  • Kelp and other seaweed
  • Coral
  • Mitigation banking and compensation

Known limits

  • Public notices often appear after a project's design is done, so some listings will be too late to influence the design.
  • Automatic listings have not been checked by a person and can be wrong.
  • Some size readings can be wrong, for example when a notice also mentions a road or pipeline length.
  • Many countries and regions are not covered yet; see the coverage table above.
  • Notices are quoted in their original language. Keyword rules in languages other than English have fewer tests, so those listings are lower confidence.

Who runs MitiMark

MitiMark is a prototype run by Asa Miller, who also works part-time for ECOncrete, a maker of eco-engineered marine products. MitiMark uses only public sources: every listing comes from a public registry or notice, and nothing comes from any company's internal information.

Feedback links on the projects go to a Google Form. This site stores nothing about you.
